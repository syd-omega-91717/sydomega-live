NET WORTH TRACKING
Stanley & Danko (1996, The Millionaire Next Door) defined wealth as: Net Worth = Assets − Liabilities. Their formula for "expected" net worth: (Age × Pre-tax Income) / 10. Prodigious accumulators of wealth (PAW) accumulate 2× expected; under-accumulators 0.5×. Tracking drives accountability.
Stanley & Danko (1996). The Millionaire Next Door
ASSET ALLOCATION
Brinson, Hood & Beebower (1986): asset allocation determines 93.6% of portfolio return variation — not stock selection or market timing. A sovereign allocation across uncorrelated asset classes (equities, bonds, real estate, alternatives) is the primary wealth-building decision.
Brinson, Hood & Beebower (1986). Financial Analysts Journal
LIQUIDITY HIERARCHY
Emergency fund theory (Blanchett, 2013): hold 3–6 months of expenses in liquid accounts before investing. Illiquidity premium — locking up capital in real estate, private equity, crypto — yields 2–5% additional return per year, but requires liquidity buffer first.
Blanchett (2013). Journal of Financial Planning
WEALTH IDENTITY
Klontz & Klontz (2009) identified four "money scripts" that govern financial behavior: money avoidance, money worship, money status, and money vigilance. Only vigilance correlates with wealth accumulation. Tracking net worth explicitly builds vigilance and detaches wealth from identity threat.
Klontz & Klontz (2009). Journal of Financial Therapy
COMPOUND INTEREST
Einstein (apocryphally) called compound interest the "eighth wonder of the world." The Rule of 72: 72 ÷ interest rate = years to double. At 7% (S&P historical average): doubles in ~10.3 years. At 10%: 7.2 years. The earlier you track and invest, the more doublings you get.
Graham, B. (1949). The Intelligent Investor
SOVEREIGN FINANCIAL INDEPENDENCE
The 4% rule (Bengen, 1994; Trinity Study, 1998): a portfolio can sustain withdrawals of 4% annually for 30+ years. FI number = Annual Expenses × 25. This is the threshold at which capital works harder than you do — the mathematical definition of financial sovereignty.
Bengen (1994) · Cooley et al. (1998). Journal of Financial Planning
