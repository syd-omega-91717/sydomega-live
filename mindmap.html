THE SCIENCE OF VISUAL THINKING
MIND MAPPING — Tony Buzan (1974)
Buzan established radial, non-linear organization as superior to sequential note-taking for memory encoding and creative generation. Mind maps activate both cerebral hemispheres simultaneously — spatial layout (right) and language (left) — creating richer, more interconnected memory traces. Buzan's research showed a 10-15% improvement in recall over linear notes under controlled conditions.
CONCEPT MAPPING — Joseph Novak (1972)
Novak developed concept mapping at Cornell as a tool for meaningful learning vs. rote memorization. Key principle: nodes represent concepts; labeled edges represent propositions. Well-formed concept maps externalize schema structure, revealing gaps in understanding and false assumptions. Novak's studies showed concept-mapped students retained information 3× longer than linear-note peers.
VISUOSPATIAL WORKING MEMORY — Baddeley (1986)
Baddeley's model of working memory identifies the visuospatial sketchpad as a dedicated system for spatial and visual information — separate from the phonological loop (verbal). Mind maps leverage this specialized channel, creating a dual-coding effect (Paivio, 1971) where verbal and spatial encoding compound recall. Information encoded in both channels is 40% more retrievable than single-channel encoding.
KNOWLEDGE EXTERNALIZATION — Nonaka & Takeuchi (1995)
Tacit knowledge — instinct, experience, and mental models — becomes actionable only when externalized into explicit representations. Mind maps are externalization engines: they convert fluid mental structures into shareable, refinable artifacts. Nonaka and Takeuchi's SECI model positions externalization as the highest-value knowledge creation activity. Organizations that systematically externalize tacit knowledge innovate faster and make better decisions.
