GOAL GRADIENT EFFECT
Kivetz et al. (2006): effort and speed increase as you approach a goal. Breaking a project into milestones simulates this — each milestone completion triggers a fresh sprint toward the next. Progress visibility is a motivational multiplier, not just a reporting tool.
Kivetz et al. (2006). Journal of Marketing Research
IMPLEMENTATION INTENTIONS
Gollwitzer (1999): specifying WHEN, WHERE, and HOW you will act increases goal completion by 200–300%. "If situation X occurs, then I will do Y" bridges the intention–action gap. Every project task should map to a specific time slot in your calendar.
Gollwitzer (1999). American Psychologist, 54(7)
PLANNING FALLACY
Kahneman & Tversky (1979): people consistently underestimate time, costs, and risks for projects. Reference class forecasting — comparing your plan to similar past projects — corrects this. Always multiply your initial time estimate by 1.5–2x as a baseline adjustment.
Kahneman & Tversky (1979). Econometrica, 47(2)
CONSTRAINT THEORY
Goldratt's Theory of Constraints (1984): every system has one binding constraint (bottleneck) that limits throughput. Optimizing non-bottlenecks yields zero system improvement. The Five Focusing Steps: Identify → Exploit → Subordinate → Elevate → Repeat.
Goldratt, E.M. (1984). The Goal
PARKINSON'S LAW
Parkinson (1955): work expands to fill the time allotted. Tight deadlines force prioritization and eliminate gold-plating. Timeboxing — fixed-duration work blocks — is the systematic application of this principle.
Parkinson, C.N. (1955). The Economist
AGILE MINIMUM VIABLE PRODUCT
Ries (2011) Lean Startup: ship the smallest thing that delivers real value and generates real feedback. Validated learning beats upfront planning. Each project iteration is a hypothesis test: Build → Measure → Learn → Repeat.
Ries, E. (2011). The Lean Startup
