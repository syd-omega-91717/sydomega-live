CIRCUMPLEX MODEL OF AFFECT
Russell, J.A. (1980) — A Circumplex Model of Affect. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 39(6)
James Russell proposed that all emotions can be plotted on two fundamental dimensions: valence (pleasure-displeasure) and arousal (activation-deactivation). This two-dimensional space explains how emotions that feel similar are close together, while opposites are far apart. The circumplex model is foundational to affective science and forms the basis of most modern mood-tracking systems.
BROADEN-AND-BUILD THEORY
Fredrickson, B.L. (2001) — The Role of Positive Emotions in Positive Psychology. American Psychologist, 56(3)
Barbara Fredrickson's Broaden-and-Build theory states that positive emotions broaden one's awareness and encourage novel, varied, and exploratory thoughts and actions. Over time, this broadened behavioral repertoire builds lasting personal resources — social bonds, knowledge, physical vitality. Tracking and cultivating positive states is not indulgence but a strategic compound investment in long-term capability.
EMOTIONAL GRANULARITY
Barrett, L.F. (2017) — How Emotions Are Made. Houghton Mifflin Harcourt
Lisa Feldman Barrett's research shows that people who can precisely distinguish between emotional states ("emotional granularity") experience less reactivity, drink less alcohol under stress, regulate emotion more effectively, and have better mental health. The sovereign practice of naming exact emotions — not just "bad" but "anxious," "frustrated," "melancholic" — is itself a regulatory intervention.
AFFECT LABELING
Lieberman, M.D. et al. (2007) — Putting Feelings into Words. Psychological Science, 18(5)
Labeling emotional experiences ("affect labeling") decreases amygdala activation and increases prefrontal regulatory control. This is the neuroscience of why journaling and mood logging work: the act of naming reduces the intensity of negative states. Putting emotions into words is a direct neurological intervention, not merely a soft-skill exercise.
MOOD FORECASTING & CALIBRATION
Wilson, T.D. & Gilbert, D.T. (2003) — Affective Forecasting. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 35
Humans systematically misjudge how long and intensely future events will affect their mood — a phenomenon called "impact bias." Longitudinal mood tracking creates a calibration database: by reviewing how predicted events actually affected your state, you develop more accurate affective forecasting, leading to better decisions about time, relationships, and effort allocation.
