TESTING EFFECT
Roediger & Karpicke (2006): retrieval practice (testing) produces dramatically better long-term retention than re-reading (study effect). Being forced to recall an answer — even incorrectly — strengthens the memory trace more than passive review. Tests are not assessments; they are the learning itself.
Roediger & Karpicke (2006). Psychological Science
SPACED REPETITION (SM-2)
Ebbinghaus (1885) forgetting curve + Wozniak (1987) SM-2 algorithm: review items at expanding intervals (1d → 6d → 14d → 28d...) calibrated by recall quality. This exploits the spacing effect and desirable difficulty principle. SM-2 reduces study time by 60–80% vs. mass practice for the same retention.
Wozniak (1987). SuperMemo SM-2 Algorithm
INTERLEAVING
Rohrer & Taylor (2007): mixing problem types (interleaving) during practice produces worse immediate performance but far better long-term retention and transfer — because it forces discrimination between concepts. Examining across multiple domains in a single session exploits this effect.
Rohrer & Taylor (2007). European Journal of Cognitive Psychology
DESIRABLE DIFFICULTY
Bjork (1994): learning is most effective when it is effortful. Generation (producing answers vs. recognizing them), spacing, and interleaving all introduce desirable difficulty. The harder a retrieval feels in the moment, the stronger the memory trace it builds. Easy practice feels productive but is not.
Bjork, R.A. (1994). Memory Distortions
METACOGNITION
Flavell (1979): metacognition — knowing what you know — is a trainable skill. People systematically overestimate their knowledge (Dunning-Kruger, 1999). Self-examination with grading forces calibration of confidence vs. accuracy, building the metacognitive awareness that separates experts from novices.
Flavell (1979) · Dunning & Kruger (1999)
DELIBERATE PRACTICE
Ericsson (1993): expert performance results from deliberate practice — focused repetition in areas of weakness with immediate feedback. Examination that identifies weak domains and routes more questions there is computational deliberate practice. 10,000 hours of random practice ≠ 10,000 hours of deliberate practice.
Ericsson et al. (1993). Psychological Review
